The real-food budget calculator

A working estimate of what a whole-food diet costs, so you can plan instead of guess. Set your household size and sourcing tier; the ledger updates live. Figures are a US-dollar baseline — adjust up or down for your country and local prices.

Budget: conventional cuts, sale meat, tinned fish, frozen produce, bulk staples.
Standard: a mix — some grass-fed and organic, pastured eggs, fresh produce.
Premium: mostly grass-fed / pasture-raised / organic and wild-caught throughout.

Estimated cost

Estimates only, based on typical whole-food prices. Real cost depends on where you shop, what's on sale, and how much you cook from scratch. The single biggest lever is cooking rather than buying prepared food — see the savings notes below.

SAVE

Buy the whole animal cheap

Chuck, whole chicken, organ meats, tinned fish and eggs are the lowest cost-per-nutrient in the store. Steak is optional; nutrition is not.

SAVE

Frozen & bulk

Frozen produce and wild fish are cheaper and just as nutritious. Buy nuts, rice and dried beans from bulk bins, not branded boxes.

SAVE

Cook it yourself

The markup on prepared and packaged food is enormous. An hour of batch cooking on the weekend is the highest-paid hour of your week.

SAVE

Warehouse & discount

Costco, Aldi, Lidl and WinCo carry clean staples at the lowest prices. Split a bulk pack with a friend to skip the storage problem.

SAVE

Eat the reductions

Meat marked down near its sell-by date is perfect for the freezer. Plan meals around what's discounted, not the other way round.

SAVE

Drink water, not calories

Cutting soda, juice, sports drinks and alcohol often pays for the entire upgrade to better meat and eggs on its own.

02

Free & clean drinking water

Clean water matters as much as clean food, and it should not cost a fortune in single-use bottles. Here is where to get safe water for free or nearly free — and how to make the water you already have cleaner.

FREE · MAPPED

Natural springs

Find a free natural spring near you at findaspring.org — a community-mapped, crowd-sourced database of springs worldwide. Bring your own glass or stainless containers. Confirm each spring is tested and currently safe before drinking.

FREE · APP

Public refill stations

Refill apps and networks map free public water-bottle refill points in most cities — libraries, parks, transit hubs, cafés. Carry a reusable bottle and use them.

FREE

Public drinking fountains

Parks, government buildings, universities and gyms almost always have free, potable fountains. In most developed countries this water is tested and safe.

LOW COST

Supermarket refill dispensers

Many grocers (Whole Foods, co-ops, and reverse-osmosis kiosks like those out front of US supermarkets) refill your own jug for a few cents a litre.

KNOW FIRST

Test your tap

In much of the world tap water is safe and free. Read your utility's annual water-quality report, or use a cheap home test kit for lead, chlorine and hardness before assuming you need to buy anything.

UPGRADE

Filter what you have

A carbon filter (jug or tap-mount) cuts chlorine and taste cheaply. Reverse osmosis removes far more (add trace minerals or a pinch of salt back). A good filter pays for itself against bottled water in weeks.

TRAVEL

Boil, or use a portable filter

Where tap water is not safe, a rolling boil for one minute makes it drinkable, and a portable filter bottle (0.1-micron or better) handles most pathogens on the move.

MINERALS

Natural mineral water

Genuine mineral water is a cheap source of magnesium and calcium. Read the label for the mineral content — the point is the minerals, not the branding.

Water safety is local. Never assume a spring, well or foreign tap is safe without a current test or local confirmation. When unsure, filter and boil, or use a sealed source.

03

Clean everyday hygiene & body care

Skin is not a barrier that keeps everything out — a good deal of what you rub on it and wash with ends up in you. The same principle as food applies: short ingredient lists, and skip the handful of additives worth avoiding. This is guidance on categories, not brand endorsement.

ProductWhat to skip on the labelWhat to choose instead
ToothpasteFluoride, artificial dyes, sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS), and high-intensity artificial sweeteners.A fluoride-free hydroxyapatite paste (e.g. Boka, RiseWell) with a short ingredient list — hydroxyapatite is the mineral teeth are actually made of, so it remineralises enamel without the fluoride.

Soap & body washLong lists of synthetic fragrance, dyes and harsh sulfates that leave skin stripped.A plain bar soap — olive, tallow or goat-milk based — with a handful of recognisable ingredients.
ShampooHeavy fragrance loads and dyes; over-stripping sulfates if your scalp is dry.A gentle shampoo with a shorter list. Wash less often; most hair does not need daily detergent.
Moisturiser & lotionSynthetic fragrance and long filler lists.Single-ingredient options work beautifully: shea butter, tallow balm, jojoba, or cold-pressed olive/coconut oil.
Sunscreen—Mineral (zinc oxide / titanium dioxide) sunscreens sit on top of the skin and are a sensible default. Sun sense — timing and shade — does the rest.
Lip balmArtificial flavour and colour, petroleum-heavy fillers.Beeswax-based balms with oils you recognise (shea, coconut, olive).
Deodorant soap for hands"Antibacterial" triclosan-type additives you don't need for everyday washing.Plain soap and water. It is as effective for daily use and far simpler.

Menstrual / intimate productsAdded fragrance and dyes.Unscented, or organic-cotton options. This is sensitive tissue — simpler is better.
Razors & shavingFoaming creams with long synthetic lists and heavy fragrance.A simple shaving oil or soap; even plain coconut oil works. Fewer ingredients, less irritation.

Build a hormone-safe shelf: what to actually use

The cleanest body care is often the most old-fashioned: rendered animal fat, plant oils, beeswax and clay. Grass-fed beef tallow in particular is close to the skin's own oils, deeply moisturising, and has nothing in it to disrupt anything. Here's a simple, natural pick for each shelf.

FACE & BODY

Whipped beef tallow balm

Grass-fed tallow (± a little olive or jojoba oil) is a complete moisturiser for face and body — rich in vitamins A, D, E and K, and closer to skin than any lotion. One jar replaces a shelf.

CLEANSE

Tallow or goat-milk soap

A simple cold-process bar — tallow, olive or coconut oil, lye, essential oil if any. No sulfates, no synthetic fragrance, doesn't strip the skin.

DEODORANT

Baking-soda or magnesium deodorant

A short-ingredient stick or cream (coconut oil, baking soda or magnesium, arrowroot, beeswax). Skips synthetic fragrance; aluminium is a personal call.

FACE CARE

Single-oil routine

Cleanse with the oil-cleansing method (castor + jojoba), moisturise with tallow or squalane. Fewer products, no "anti-ageing" fragrance cocktails.

HAIR

Gentle wash + oil

A short-list shampoo used less often; condition ends with a drop of jojoba or argan oil. Most scalps do not need daily detergent.

LIPS & SHAVE

Beeswax balm, oil shave

Beeswax-and-oil lip balm; shave with a plain oil or tallow soap. Both beat the foaming, fragranced versions for irritation.

The ingredients that can mess with hormones — skip these

On the labelUsually found inWhy skip it
"Fragrance" / "Parfum"Almost everything scentedA legal catch-all that can hide dozens of undisclosed compounds, including phthalates. The single most common thing to avoid.
Phthalates (DEP, DBP)Fragrance, nail products, hair sprayStudied as endocrine disruptors; often hidden inside "fragrance," so unscented is the safer default.
Parabens (methyl-, propyl-…)Lotions, shampoos, creamsPreservatives that can weakly mimic oestrogen. Easy to avoid — many products are now "paraben-free."
OxybenzoneChemical sunscreensAbsorbs into skin and is a hormone-disruption concern. Choose mineral (zinc / titanium) sunscreen instead.

General guidance for choosing simpler products, not medical or safety advice, and not a claim that any single ingredient will harm a given person. The evidence on "endocrine disruptors" varies by dose and compound and is still developing — the sensible move is a short, legible ingredient list and skipping synthetic fragrance where you can. If you have a skin condition or allergy, follow a dermatologist's advice over any list. Patch-test new products (including tallow) first.

04

Clean clothing & natural fibers

The same idea as the food aisle: most of what's on the rack is engineered to be cheap, not to be worn against your skin all day. Polyester, nylon and acrylic are plastic — spun from petroleum — and they now turn up blended into almost everything, even "cotton" basics and socks. The fix is exactly the food fix: flip it over and read the tag. Aim for as close to 100% natural fiber as you can, and skip the pure-plastic garments.

CHOOSE

Natural fibers

Cotton (organic if you can), linen, hemp, merino & other wool, silk, cashmere. Breathable, they last, and they don't shed plastic into your water or the ocean.

CAUTION

"Semi-synthetics"

Rayon / viscose, modal, and especially "bamboo" — which is almost always rayon, a plant chemically dissolved into fibre. Tencel/lyocell is the cleaner-made version. Fine, but not the same as a true natural fibre.

SKIP / LIMIT

Plastics

Polyester, nylon, acrylic, olefin, and spandex/elastane. A few % elastane for stretch (socks, waistbands) is hard to avoid — the goal is highest natural %, not chasing a perfect 100 on stretchy items.

What you're buyingThe blend to skipWhat to choose instead
T-shirts & basics"Cotton/poly" blends (the Walmart/Hanes default is now cotton mixed with polyester).100% cotton — check the tag says 100, not "60/40". Organic cotton (Pact, Harvest & Mill), heavyweight cotton (American Giant), or hemp tees (Jungmaven).
UnderwearPolyester/spandex "performance" pairs and most fast-fashion multipacks.100% cotton (Cottonique, Pact, Organic Basics) or merino/hemp (WAMA). This is the layer that matters most — keep it natural.
SocksAll-polyester "athletic" socks — and note even good cotton socks usually carry a little nylon/elastane for durability.Highest cotton or wool % you can find: Maggie's Organics (100% organic cotton), Zkano, or merino (Darn Tough, Ridge). A small % nylon at the heel/toe is a fair trade for socks that last.
Base layers & activewear100% polyester "moisture-wicking" gym gear — the worst offender for heat, odour and microplastic shedding.Merino wool — naturally temperature-regulating and odour-resistant (Ridge Merino, Woolx, Icebreaker). It out-performs synthetic "performance" fabric at its own job.
Jeans & trousers"Stretch" denim (that stretch is elastane/polyester woven through).Rigid 100% cotton denim (Levi's rigid/non-stretch, Buck Mason) and cotton or linen chinos.
Sweaters & layersAcrylic knits (cheap plastic that pills and traps odour).Wool, merino, cashmere, cotton or linen knits. They're warmer, breathe, and last years.
Bed sheets & towels"Microfiber" (polyester) sheets and poly-blend towels.100% cotton (percale or sateen) or linen sheets; 100% cotton towels. You spend a third of your life in bed — treat it like underwear.
Any "performance" finishLabels boasting wrinkle-free, no-iron, stain-resistant, water-repellent or moisture-wicking.Skip the finish. These treatments are often PFAS ("forever chemicals") or formaldehyde-based resins bonded to the fabric — the textile version of a health claim on a box.

Read the tag in five seconds

Every garment sold has a fibre-content label sewn into the seam or collar. That number is the law, the same way the ingredient list is. 100% cotton / linen / wool = buy. A blend with 5–10% elastane on something stretchy = fine. "60% cotton, 40% polyester" on a basic tee = put it back — that's a plastic tee wearing a cotton costume. New clothes of any fibre are worth a first wash before wearing.

The honest case for natural fibres is comfort, breathability, durability, and far less plastic-microfibre shedding into water and air — all real and well documented. The stronger "wearing polyester harms your hormones" claim is not well established and we won't overstate it; treat this as choosing the better material, the same way you choose the better food. Fibre content and finishes are on the garment's own tag — verify there.
