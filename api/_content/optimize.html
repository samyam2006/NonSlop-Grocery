The avoid list is the defence. This is the offence: the small number of real foods that do the most for how you feel, think and perform — and exactly where to find them.

01

The foundation: eat these on repeat

Ninety per cent of a good diet is boring in the best way — the same handful of nutrient-dense foods, most days. These are the ones that carry the most nutrition per bite and per dollar.

Ruminant meat
Beef and lamb are the most complete protein-and-micronutrient package available: heme iron, zinc, B12, creatine and fat-soluble vitamins in the form the body uses most easily. The workhorse of the whole diet.
Eggs
The closest thing to a multivitamin that comes in its own shell — choline, B-vitamins, quality protein and every fat-soluble vitamin, mostly in the yolk. Pasture-raised where you can.
Organ meats
Liver is nature's most concentrated source of vitamin A, B12, folate, copper and iron. A few ounces a week does more than a shelf of supplements. Start small; the flavour grows on you.
Wild & oily fish
Sardines, mackerel, salmon and herring supply omega-3s (EPA/DHA), vitamin D and iodine — the exact nutrients most Western diets are short on. Tinned counts and is cheap.
Full-fat dairy
Butter, ghee, cheese, yogurt and, if you tolerate it, whole or raw milk: calcium, K2, quality fat and protein. Full-fat and unsweetened is the only version worth buying.
Traditional fats
Butter, tallow, ghee, extra-virgin olive oil and coconut oil. Stable to cook with, rich in fat-soluble vitamins, and the direct replacement for every seed oil in your kitchen.
Low-toxin plants & fruit
Leafy greens, cruciferous veg, berries, avocados, squash and root vegetables. Colour, fibre and polyphenols — eaten with fat so the fat-soluble vitamins actually absorb.
Fermented foods
Sauerkraut, kimchi, real yogurt, kefir and traditionally-fermented pickles: live cultures that feed the gut, made by time rather than a factory.

This is general education, not medical or dietary advice. Individual needs vary; if you have a medical condition, allergy, or are pregnant, talk to a qualified professional before changing your diet.

02

Eating for hormonal health & testosterone

Hormones are built from raw materials. Chronically under-eat fat, protein, and a few key minerals — as most processed diets do — and the machinery has nothing to work with. The levers below are the ones food can actually pull.

RAW MATERIAL

Enough dietary fat & cholesterol

Sex hormones are synthesised from cholesterol. Extremely low-fat diets tend to lower testosterone. Eggs, red meat, butter and full-fat dairy supply the building blocks.

MINERAL

Zinc

A well-studied lever for testosterone, and one many diets fall short on. Densest in oysters, red meat, and pumpkin seeds.

MINERAL

Magnesium

Involved in hundreds of processes including hormone regulation and sleep quality. Found in leafy greens, pumpkin seeds, dark chocolate and mineral water.

VITAMIN

Vitamin D (and sun)

Functions more like a hormone than a vitamin, and is tied to testosterone status. From oily fish, egg yolks, and sensible sun exposure.

GLAND-TO-GLAND

Boron & the fat-solubles

Boron (from raisins, prunes, avocado, nuts) and vitamins A and K2 from liver, egg yolk and butter support the endocrine system's supply chain.

PROTEIN

Adequate quality protein

Muscle is hormonally active tissue. Enough complete protein — meat, eggs, fish, dairy — plus resistance training is the strongest real-food lever there is.

AVOID

Cut the seed oil & sugar

Chronically high sugar and industrial seed oil drive the inflammation and body-fat gain that work against healthy hormones. The avoid list is part of the protocol.

FOUNDATION

Sleep & body composition

No food out-performs poor sleep and excess body fat. Most of the testosterone conversation is downstream of these two. Food supports them; it does not replace them.

Food supports normal hormonal function within a healthy lifestyle; it is not a treatment. If you suspect a clinical hormone issue, get bloodwork and see a doctor — no grocery list substitutes for a diagnosis.

03

Eating for mental clarity & a steady mood

The brain is roughly sixty per cent fat and an energy-hungry organ that punishes blood-sugar chaos. Clear thinking is less about a magic nootropic than about giving it the right fats, steady fuel, and the micronutrients it runs on.

STRUCTURE

Omega-3 fats (DHA)

DHA is a structural fat of the brain. Oily fish, fish roe and pastured egg yolks supply it directly — the single most impactful change for many people.

FUEL

Stable blood sugar

The fog and the 3pm crash are usually a sugar rollercoaster. Protein and fat at every meal — and skipping the liquid sugar — flatten it out.

METHYLATION

Choline & B-vitamins

Choline (egg yolk, liver) builds the neurotransmitter acetylcholine; B12 and folate (meat, liver, greens) keep the nervous system running.

CALM

Magnesium

Linked to sleep, stress resilience and mood. Leafy greens, pumpkin seeds and a square of dark chocolate are easy sources.

GUT-BRAIN

Fermented foods & fibre

The gut makes much of the body's serotonin. Real fermented foods and fibrous plants feed the microbes that keep the gut-brain axis calm.

HYDRATION

Water & electrolytes

Mild dehydration reads as brain fog and headache. Clean water plus sodium, potassium and magnesium keeps signalling sharp — see the water guide.

POLYPHENOLS

Berries, olive oil, cacao

Deeply coloured plants and real cocoa carry polyphenols associated with better long-term cognitive health. Colour on the plate is not decoration.

AVOID

Alcohol & ultra-processed food

Both are reliably tied to worse sleep, mood and focus. The fastest "nootropic" for most people is subtraction, not addition.

05

What a day actually looks like

Proof that none of this is exotic or expensive. A full day built entirely from the foundation foods above — no supplements, no specialty store required.

On waking
Water with a pinch of salt and a squeeze of lemon. Coffee or tea black, or with a splash of real cream.
Breakfast
Three pastured eggs cooked in butter, with sauerkraut or half an avocado. If hungrier, add a sardine tin or leftover meat.
Lunch
A palm-sized portion of beef, lamb or salmon, a large pile of greens dressed in olive oil, and some fruit or a few nuts.
The weekly extras
Liver once a week (blended into ground beef if the taste is new to you); oily fish two or three times; fermented food most days.
Dinner
Any real protein, any vegetable roasted in tallow or butter, a starch you tolerate (potato, rice, squash) if you're active. Full-fat yogurt with berries if you want something sweet.
The rule of thumb
Protein and fat at every meal, colour on the plate, nothing you can't pronounce, and water instead of anything sweet. That's the whole protocol.
06

Buying good halal meat

Halal and high-quality are not the same thing — and the goal is both. A stamp on the packet tells you how the animal was slaughtered, not how it was raised or what went into the meat afterwards. Here's how to buy halal and real.

FIRST

Hand-slaughtered, not just "certified"

Look for genuinely hand-slaughtered (dhabiha) meat where the animal was not mechanically processed. Small halal butchers and farm-direct suppliers are usually more transparent than a supermarket sticker.

RAISING

Ask how it was raised

Halal says nothing about grass-fed vs grain-fed or antibiotics. Ask the butcher: grass-fed and pasture-raised halal exists and is worth seeking out — the same quality bar as the rest of this guide.

BEST BUYS

The cuts to prioritise

Halal butchers are the cheapest reliable source of organ meats (liver, heart, kidney), oxtail, lamb, mutton, bone-in cuts and bones for broth — exactly the nutrient-dense, lower-cost cuts this guide is built on.

WATCH

What to still check

Read labels on processed halal items (sausages, nuggets, deli meat) — "halal" doesn't stop a product being full of fillers, seed oil, added sugar or phosphates. The ingredient-list rules still apply.

Where to find quality halal

SourceWhat to get there
