The Introduction

Why real food became the hard thing to find

There was a time, and it was not so long ago, when buying food did not require a degree in label-reading. Bread was flour, water, salt and time. Meat came from an animal that ate what its species evolved to eat. A tomato tasted like a tomato because it had been allowed to ripen in soil and sun rather than in a truck. Nobody spoke of "clean eating," because there was very little of the other kind to distinguish it from.

That world was dismantled deliberately, and quickly. Over roughly two generations, the majority of what fills a modern grocery store stopped being food in the old sense and became something closer to a product: a shelf-stable, brightly-packaged, precisely-engineered arrangement of refined starch, industrial seed oil, added sugar and additives whose only job is to make you eat past the point of fullness and come back for more. The industry has a term for the sweet spot where fat, salt and sugar overwhelm the brain's ability to say enough. They call it the bliss point, and they pay laboratories to find it.

None of this is a conspiracy theory; it is a business model, printed openly in quarterly reports. A box of cereal costs pennies to make and sells for several dollars precisely because the cheap ingredients — subsidised corn, wheat and soy — have been transformed into something that no longer spoils, no longer satisfies, and no longer resembles the plant it came from. The genius of it is that the packaging then sells the very virtue it destroyed: "whole grain," "all natural," "a good source of fibre," "supports immune health." The health claim on the front of the box exists in inverse proportion to the health inside it. If a food needs to advertise that it is good for you, that is usually the first clue that it is not.

The consequences are not abstract. Rates of obesity, type-2 diabetes, fatty liver, depression, infertility and the quiet epidemic of low energy and mental fog have all climbed in near-perfect step with the share of the diet made up of ultra-processed food. Correlation is not proof, but when the same curve repeats across every country that adopts the Western industrial diet — and reverses when people leave it — the honest response is not to keep waiting for a headline. It is to stop eating the product and start eating the food.

That is the entire premise of this guide. We are not selling a diet, a supplement line, a meal-delivery subscription or a fourteen-day transformation. We take no advertising and no affiliate commissions, which means we have no reason to tell you that a particular yogurt is healthy when it carries more sugar than a chocolate bar. Our only position is this: the closer a food is to the state it existed in on a farm, the better it tends to be for you — and almost everything you need to eat that way is already sitting in the store you shop at, usually around the outer walls, often cheaper than the branded imitations two aisles in.

What follows is organised the way you would actually use it. There is a directory of 141 real grocery stores across seven regions, with a note on what to reach for in each. There is a blunt list of what to avoid no matter what the packaging promises, and an exposé of the "health foods" and trusted brands that are quietly working against you. There is guidance on what to eat for genuine, measurable outcomes — steady energy, hormonal health, mental clarity — and a map of the world's real superfoods and where to buy them without paying a wellness-industry markup. And because eating well is a practical problem, not a moral one, there is a budget calculator, a list of clean everyday-hygiene products, and a directory of free, safe drinking water.

Read it in any order. But read the next part first, because everything else rests on it.

01

Seven rules that replace a thousand diets

Memorise these and you can throw away every trend that will be sold to you next year. None of them require an app, a subscription, or a single word on a package.

  1. Shop the edges of the store

    Meat, fish, eggs, dairy and produce live on the perimeter. The centre aisles are where food goes to be turned into product. Spend ninety per cent of your money and time around the walls.

  2. If it has an ingredient list longer than your hand, put it back

    Real food is mostly one ingredient. A short list of things you recognise is fine. A long list of things you cannot pronounce is a formulation, not a meal.

  3. Eat the animal, not the imitation of it

    Ruminant meat, eggs and fish are the most nutrient-dense foods available to a human. Nothing engineered in a factory to look like them is an upgrade. It is a cost-cutting substitute wearing a health costume.

  4. Fat is not the enemy; refined seed oil is

    Butter, tallow, ghee, olive oil and coconut oil have fed humans for millennia. Industrial seed oils — soybean, canola, "vegetable" — are a twentieth-century invention. Cook with the old fats.

  5. Sugar is the constant, under every alias

    It hides as cane juice, corn syrup, "fruit concentrate," maltodextrin, dextrose and thirty other names. The dose is what matters, and the dose in processed food is enormous. Track it and it collapses.

  6. Buy the cheapest version of the real thing before the expensive version of the fake thing

    Conventional eggs beat a "plant-based" egg substitute. Regular ground beef beats an ultra-processed meat analogue. Clean eating is not a rich person's hobby; the base ingredients are among the cheapest calories in the store.

  7. Distrust the health claim, trust the ingredient list

    The front of the package is marketing. The back is the law. When they disagree — and they usually do — the back is telling the truth.
