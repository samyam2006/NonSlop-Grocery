Methodology
& sources.

A guide that tells you to distrust marketing claims has to hold itself to a higher standard. Here is how the guide was built, how we frame its claims, and the evidence behind them.

01

How the guide was built

The store directory
The 141 chains were compiled from each retailer's own public information — their private-label organic/clean lines, butcher-counter and welfare programmes, and stocked brands — cross-referenced against what they publish about sourcing. Guidance describes each chain's better-sourced lines, not every product on its shelves. Retail ranges change constantly, so every store entry tells you to confirm on the package and verify claims (grass-fed, organic, wild-caught) locally.
The "avoid" and label claims
The ingredient decoder and marketing-term breakdowns describe standard, documented food-industry labelling practice (ingredient splitting, "natural"/"made with" claims, seed-oil blends). They are checks on the label, not accusations against any single named brand.
The nutrition guidance
The "eat to optimise" content reflects the weight of nutrition research on whole vs. ultra-processed foods, and on specific nutrients and where diets commonly fall short. Where evidence is strong we say so; where it is contested (for example the debate on omega-6 seed oils) we flag it rather than overstate it.
What this is not
It is educational, not medical or dietary advice, and not a treatment for any condition. Food supports normal function within a healthy lifestyle; it does not replace a diagnosis, bloodwork or a professional. Individual needs vary — use it as a starting framework, not a prescription.
Updates
Sourcing, brands and the science all move. This is a living guide; buyers get updates as the directory and evidence are refreshed. Last reviewed on publication of Volume One.
02

The evidence, by topic

Representative, credible sources for the guide's main claims. This is a reading list to stand on, not an exhaustive systematic review.

Ultra-processed food
Hall et al., “Ultra-Processed Diets Cause Excess Calorie Intake and Weight Gain,” Cell Metabolism (2019) — the NIH controlled-feeding trial. Monteiro et al., the NOVA classification (Public Health Nutrition). Srour et al., ultra-processed food and cardiovascular risk, BMJ (2019).
Added sugar
World Health Organization, Guideline: Sugars Intake for Adults and Children (2015). American Heart Association guidance on added sugars. Reviews on sugar-sweetened beverages and metabolic disease.
Seed / “vegetable” oils (contested)
The debate centres on omega-6 linoleic acid and oxidation. See reviews on dietary linoleic acid and inflammation (both cautionary and reassuring exist) — the honest position is that industrial, highly-processed oils in ultra-processed food are worth minimising, while the isolated “seed oils cause disease” claim is not settled. We frame it that way deliberately.
Produce & pesticide residue
Environmental Working Group, Shopper's Guide to Pesticide in Produce (Dirty Dozen / Clean Fifteen), updated annually from USDA and FDA residue testing.
Zinc & testosterone
Prasad et al., zinc status and testosterone in men (Nutrition, 1996) — effects are clearest in correcting deficiency, not as a booster in already-replete men.
Vitamin D & testosterone
Pilz et al., vitamin D supplementation and testosterone levels (Hormone and Metabolic Research, 2011); associations are strongest where vitamin D status is low.
Dietary fat & hormones
Reviews and trials showing very-low-fat diets can modestly lower testosterone versus higher-fat diets (e.g., Whittaker & Wu meta-analysis, J. Steroid Biochemistry & Molecular Biology, 2021).
Omega-3 (DHA/EPA) & the brain
Established literature on DHA as a structural brain fatty acid and EPA/DHA in mood and cognition; oily fish as the primary dietary source.
Fermented foods & the gut
Wastyk et al., “Gut-microbiota-targeted diets modulate human immune status,” Cell (2021) — the Stanford trial in which a fermented-food diet increased microbiome diversity and lowered inflammatory markers.
Body care & endocrine disruptors
Reviews on phthalates, parabens and oxybenzone as endocrine-active compounds; the U.S. FDA's ongoing review of sunscreen active-ingredient absorption. Evidence varies by dose and compound — minimise exposure to synthetic fragrance as the simplest hedge.

Citations are provided so you can read the primary sources yourself. Science evolves; where studies conflict, we've tried to represent the balance rather than cherry-pick. Nothing here is medical advice.
